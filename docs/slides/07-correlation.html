Can’t We Fix This With Better Phrasing?

Samuel J. Gershman and Tomer D. Ullman “Causal Implicatures from Correlational Statements,” PLOS ONE 18, no. 5 (May 18, 2023): e0286067, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286067.

  • People infer causation from correlational statements, using the variable order as a directional indicator

    • Even with “X is associated with an increased risk of Y” language
    • Even when given the choice of non-causal interpretations, 47% of people still use causal interpretations

In summary, certain correlational statements are associated with an increased probability of causal implicature. To be clear, we are not implying that these correlational statements cause causal implicature, but rather that they are correlated with causal implicature. In other words, correlation does not imply causation, but it does sometimes “imply” causation.

Mental Strategies to Counter Correlation “Implies” Causation

Check the experimental design:

  • Random assignment of treatments \(\rightarrow\)
    Causal inferences are ok!

  • Representative sample of the population \(\rightarrow\)
    Generalization is ok!

If it’s not an experiment with random assignment,
consider other possible explanatory factors

  • Could causality run the other way?

  • Are there “lurking” variables that might explain some of the association?

Spurious Correlations

Be suspicious if

  1. There aren’t many data points and/or data points have been cherry-picked in some way
  2. There are a large number of possible variables to compare
  3. Data were gathered from different places (not a deal-breaker, just reason for caution)

Example: 2008 Recession Economic Policy

Economists Reinhart and Rogoff published a paper, Growth in a Time of Debt (2010), arguing:

median growth rates for countries with public debt over 90 percent of GDP are roughly one percent lower than otherwise; average (mean) growth rates are several percent lower.

Issues:

  • Years of high debt and average growth were selectively excluded from the data set
  • Countries’ GDP growth rates were not properly weighted
  • Summary table excludes high-debt and average-growth countries
